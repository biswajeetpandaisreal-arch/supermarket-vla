\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter moves from the general to the specific: from generalist robot policies
(\refsec{sec:bg-vla}) and the model used here (\refsec{sec:bg-smolvla}), through the
techniques that make it trainable on modest hardware (\refsec{sec:bg-efficient}), to
the learning paradigm (\refsec{sec:bg-il}), the physical skill
(\refsec{sec:bg-grasp}), the embodiment (\refsec{sec:bg-mobile}) and the linguistic
capability (\refsec{sec:bg-lang}) the task demands. It then examines the two areas the
results turn out to depend on most --- the training data (\refsec{sec:bg-data}) and how
performance is measured (\refsec{sec:bg-eval}) --- before stating the gap this work
addresses (\refsec{sec:bg-gap}).

\section{Scope and method of the review}
\label{sec:bg-scope}
The review covers four intersecting literatures: VLA models and generalist robot
policies; imitation learning for manipulation; language grounding for embodied agents;
and the simulation, benchmarking and evaluation practices used to assess such systems.
Sources were gathered from the major robotics and machine-learning venues (CoRL, RSS,
ICRA, IROS, NeurIPS, ICLR, ICML, and the journals T-RO, RA-L and IJRR) together with
arXiv preprints, which in this fast-moving field frequently precede or replace formal
publication. Searches combined the terms \emph{vision-language-action}, \emph{robot
foundation model}, \emph{imitation learning}, \emph{action chunking},
\emph{language-conditioned manipulation}, \emph{behaviour cloning} and
\emph{sim-to-real}, supplemented by citation tracing from the survey of
\textcite{sapkota2026vla} and the SmolVLA report \parencite{shukor2025smolvla}.

Two boundaries apply. The review is weighted toward work from 2022 onward, since the
VLA framing dates only to 2023 \parencite{zitkovich2023rt2}; older work is included
where it establishes a concept still in use, such as behaviour cloning
\parencite{pomerleau1988alvinn}. Reinforcement learning is treated only where it bears
on imitation learning, since no reward signal is used in this thesis.

\section{Vision--Language--Action models}
\label{sec:bg-vla}
Classical robot learning trains one policy per task: given enough demonstrations
of ``pick up the red cube'', a policy learns that behaviour and nothing else.
Scaling this way requires a dedicated dataset for every task, object and
environment --- a combinatorial explosion that slow, physical, real-robot data
collection cannot keep up with. Large language models and vision--language models
(VLMs) solved an analogous generalisation problem for text and images by training
one large model on internet-scale data so that it can follow novel instructions.
\emph{Vision--Language--Action} models take that same pretrained vision--language
backbone and extend its output space from text to robot actions, so that broad,
web-scale visual and linguistic understanding can be used to decide what a robot
should do next \parencite{zitkovich2023rt2, sapkota2026vla}.

A VLA is typically built from three parts \parencite{sapkota2026vla}: a
\emph{vision encoder} that turns images into visual features; a \emph{language
model} that reads the instruction and reasons over image and text, usually
inherited wholesale from a pretrained VLM; and an \emph{action decoder} (or action
expert) that turns the fused representation into low-level robot actions. Three
design choices recur across modern VLAs and matter directly for this work.

\subsection{Action chunking.} Rather than predicting one action per timestep, the
policy predicts a short sequence (a ``chunk'') of future actions in a single
forward pass, then executes some or all of that chunk before re-planning. Action
chunking was introduced by ACT and shown to enable fine-grained behaviours by
modelling temporally consistent action sequences
\parencite{zhao2023act, wang2026vlalimits}. The number of chunk steps actually
executed before re-planning (the execution horizon) has a strong, non-monotonic
effect on success: too short sacrifices smoothness, too long sacrifices
reactivity \parencite{wang2026vlalimits}. The mechanism is now near-universal ---
in diffusion policies \parencite{chi2023diffusionpolicy}, flow-matching VLAs
\parencite{black2024pi0, shukor2025smolvla} and even reinforcement learning
\parencite{li2025qchunking}. Because the chunk is an explicit multi-step plan, it can
also be read out directly as a window onto the policy's intention
(\refsec{sec:results-interpret}).

\subsection{Pretrain-then-post-train.} A VLA's backbone is pretrained once on broad,
non-robot data (or an existing VLM checkpoint), then \emph{post-trained}
(fine-tuned) on a much smaller robot-specific dataset for the target embodiment and
task \parencite{xiang2026posttraining}. This two-stage recipe is the whole point
of using a VLA rather than training from scratch: the model arrives already
knowing what objects and language look like, and need only learn the motor
mapping. This directly motivates the frozen-backbone strategy in
\refChap{chap:methodology}.

\subsection{Action representation.} Early VLAs discretised each action dimension into
bins and emitted them as text tokens, reducing action prediction to next-token
prediction \parencite{zitkovich2023rt2, kim2024openvla}. This needs no new output head
but is coarse, because continuous joint targets are quantised, and slow, because a
chunk of $H$ actions over $d$ dimensions costs $H\times d$ decoding steps. The
alternative is a continuous action head trained as a conditional generative model:
denoising diffusion \parencite{chi2023diffusionpolicy} or a flow-matching field
\parencite{lipman2023flowmatching, black2024pi0, shukor2025smolvla}. These emit a whole
continuous chunk in a few integration steps, which suits fine manipulation
(\refsec{sec:bg-flow}).

\subsection{A short lineage}
\label{sec:bg-lineage}
Following \textcite{sapkota2026vla}, four overlapping generations can be traced.

\subsubsection{Precursors (2021--2022).} Early systems combined pretrained
vision--language embeddings with hand-designed primitives. CLIPort attached CLIP's
semantic representation \parencite{radford2021clip} to a spatial manipulation head,
separating ``what'' from ``where'' \parencite{shridhar2021cliport}; SayCan used a
language model to select among a fixed skill library, weighting each candidate by a
learned affordance \parencite{ahn2022saycan}. Language operated \emph{above} a fixed
repertoire of controllers rather than inside the controller itself.

\subsubsection{Transformer policies (2022--2023).} RT-1 trained a transformer end to
end on 130{,}000 real teleoperated episodes, showing a single network could absorb a
large multi-task dataset \parencite{brohan2023rt1}. ACT introduced action chunking for
fine-grained manipulation from only tens of demonstrations \parencite{zhao2023act}, and
Diffusion Policy showed that modelling the action distribution generatively resolves
the multimodality that makes behaviour cloning brittle
\parencite{chi2023diffusionpolicy}. RT-2 then coined the ``VLA'' framing, fine-tuning a
large VLM directly on robot trajectories and reporting emergent capabilities no purely
robot-trained policy exhibited \parencite{zitkovich2023rt2}.

\subsubsection{Open models and shared data (2023--2024).} Open X-Embodiment pooled data
from 22 embodiments and showed positive transfer across them
\parencite{padalkar2024openx}. Octo \parencite{ghosh2024octo} and OpenVLA
\parencite{kim2024openvla} released open-weight generalist policies trained on that
corpus, making VLA research reproducible outside industrial laboratories --- a
precondition for work such as this thesis.

\subsubsection{Flow-matching and efficiency-focused models (2024--2026).} $\pi_0$
replaced token decoding with a flow-matching action expert \parencite{black2024pi0},
and $\pi_{0.5}$ extended this to open-world generalisation by co-training on
heterogeneous sources, with target-embodiment demonstrations only a small fraction of
the mixture \parencite{black2025pi05}. GR00T~N1 applied a dual-system reasoning/control
split to humanoids \parencite{nvidia2025gr00t}, and Gemini Robotics brought a frontier
VLM into the same framing \parencite{deepmind2025gemini}. Alongside this scaling trend
runs a smaller literature concerned with cost: TinyVLA argues a compact backbone with a
diffusion head can match far larger models on individual tasks
\parencite{wen2025tinyvla}, and later work shows fine-tuning recipe choices affect speed
and success as much as parameter count \parencite{kim2025oft}. SmolVLA belongs to this
generation \parencite{shukor2025smolvla}.

For a project constrained to a single consumer GPU, every model in the fourth
generation except the efficiency-focused ones is out of reach --- not merely slow to
fine-tune but impossible to fine-tune at all within \SI{12}{GB}. The practical question
is therefore what the \emph{smallest} models in this lineage can and cannot do, which
the literature answers only partially (\refsec{sec:bg-gap}).

\section{SmolVLA}
\label{sec:bg-smolvla}
SmolVLA is a compact (approximately 450~million parameter) open VLA distributed
through Hugging Face LeRobot, designed for efficient fine-tuning and deployment on
modest hardware \parencite{shukor2025smolvla, cadene2024lerobot}. It combines a
compact pretrained VLM (SmolVLM-2, \parencite{marafioti2025smolvlm}) with a
flow-matching action expert, processing multi-image and language inputs to produce
action chunks. It uses visual-token reduction and a layer-skipping technique that lets
the action expert read features from an intermediate VLM layer; the action expert
interleaves cross-attention and causal self-attention layers
\parencite{kiviranta2025smolvla, shukor2025smolvla}. Its pretraining corpus is
community-contributed data from low-cost arms rather than an industrial fleet, which is
part of the same affordability argument \parencite{shukor2025smolvla}.

The \SI{12}{GB} ceiling constrained model selection throughout: it ruled out the
multi-billion-parameter families outright and, as \refChap{chap:methodology} describes,
also ruled out unfreezing the vision backbone once three camera streams were in use. No
claim is made that SmolVLA is the \emph{largest} model that would fit; the one larger
candidate trialled, $\pi_0$, could not be brought up for reasons unrelated to memory
(\refsec{sec:results-journey}), so the question was never settled empirically.

Relative to the discrete-token approach of RT-2 \parencite{zitkovich2023rt2} and
OpenVLA \parencite{kim2024openvla}, SmolVLA's flow-matching head produces continuous
chunks in a single pass --- faster at inference and better suited to fine-grained
motion. Relative to $\pi_{0}/\pi_{0.5}$ and GR00T
\parencite{black2024pi0, black2025pi05, nvidia2025gr00t}, it sacrifices peak capability
for a large reduction in size. That trade is exactly what this thesis interrogates:
whether the smaller model is nonetheless \emph{sufficient} for a controlled,
language-conditioned task under a hard hardware ceiling.

Kiviranta's bachelor's thesis fine-tunes the same checkpoint on the low-cost SO-101 arm
and reports an 80\% success rate on a single task from around 100 demonstrations, while
noting that sequential multi-task fine-tuning caused catastrophic forgetting and that
language conditioning functioned more as a proof of concept than a working
instruction-following system \parencite{kiviranta2025smolvla}. As far as this review
found, it is the only independent published evaluation of SmolVLA under a low-budget
single-task regime, which makes it the closest available comparison despite differing in
embodiment and in using real hardware rather than simulation.

\section{Efficient adaptation under a hardware constraint}
\label{sec:bg-efficient}
Because the hardware ceiling is a first-class constraint here, the techniques for
adapting a large pretrained model on a small device deserve their own treatment.

\subsection{Freezing.} The simplest option is to update only part of the network,
usually holding the vision--language backbone fixed and training only the action
expert. This eliminates optimiser state and gradients for the frozen parameters and,
more importantly, avoids storing backbone activations for the backward pass. The
argument is not only memory: a backbone pretrained on large image--text corpora already
encodes the structure the task needs, and back-propagating into it from a few hundred
demonstrations risks overwriting that prior --- the catastrophic forgetting
characterised by \textcite{kirkpatrick2017ewc} and observed for VLA fine-tuning by
\textcite{kiviranta2025smolvla}. The counter-argument is that a frozen backbone cannot
adapt to a distribution it was not trained on, which matters when images are synthetic
renders (\refsec{sec:bg-sim}).

\subsection{Low-rank adaptation.} LoRA inserts trainable low-rank matrices alongside
frozen weights, giving a middle path at a fraction of the memory cost
\parencite{hu2022lora}; QLoRA adds 4-bit quantisation of the frozen weights
\parencite{dettmers2023qlora}. OpenVLA's released recipe uses LoRA for new-embodiment
fine-tuning \parencite{kim2024openvla}. LoRA is not used here --- freezing sufficed ---
but low-rank adaptation of the \emph{vision} layers is exactly what the sim-to-real
argument of \refsec{sec:bg-sim} suggests, and is discussed as future work.

\subsection{Architectural efficiency.} Rather than adapting a large model cheaply, one
can design a small one. TinyVLA pairs a sub-billion-parameter backbone with a diffusion
head at a fraction of the training and inference cost \parencite{wen2025tinyvla};
SmolVLA reduces visual tokens and skips the upper half of the VLM at inference
\parencite{shukor2025smolvla}. These techniques are what make the present work possible.

\subsection{Recipe choices.} \textcite{kim2025oft} report that action head, number of
input views and decoding scheme affect throughput and success substantially, sometimes
more than model size does --- the same kind of point that \refsec{sec:bg-data} and
\refsec{sec:bg-eval} make about data and metrics.

\section{ACT: an imitation-learning baseline}
\label{sec:bg-act}
ACT (Action Chunking Transformer) is named precisely because it serves both as the
baseline in the preliminary study (\refsec{sec:results-journey}) and as a same-task
capacity control (\refsec{sec:results-act}). ACT is a \emph{from-scratch}
imitation-learning policy, \emph{not} a VLA: it has no pretrained language backbone and
cannot accept a free-form text instruction. It is trained end-to-end on a single task's
demonstrations and predicts action chunks directly from camera images and joint state,
using a conditional variational autoencoder to model the demonstrator's variability
\parencite{zhao2023act}. It therefore represents the classical, no-pretraining approach
against which a VLA is compared.

The closest alternative baseline is Diffusion Policy
\parencite{chi2023diffusionpolicy}, which shares ACT's chunked formulation but models
the action distribution by denoising diffusion rather than by regression. That
difference is not merely technical. ACT's reconstruction loss is $L1$, which is
median-seeking: when the data contain several distinct behaviours consistent with the
same observation, an $L1$-trained policy collapses onto one rather than averaging them.
Diffusion and flow-matching objectives instead represent the full distribution
\parencite{chi2023diffusionpolicy, lipman2023flowmatching}. This is directly visible in
the results: trained on four items with no way to tell them apart, ACT collapses onto
the single most reliable behaviour (\refsec{sec:results-act}) --- exactly what the
objective predicts. ACT was chosen over Diffusion Policy because it is the baseline used
in the preliminary study, because SmolVLA inherits its chunking mechanism, and because
it is supported directly in LeRobot, allowing an identical pipeline for both.

\section{Imitation learning and behaviour cloning}
\label{sec:bg-il}
Both policies studied here are trained by \emph{imitation learning}, specifically
\emph{behaviour cloning}: a policy $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{o})$ is fitted to
reproduce the actions in a fixed dataset of (observation, action) pairs from a
demonstrator --- here a scripted inverse-kinematics controller --- without any reward
signal or environment interaction during training. The approach dates to ALVINN
\parencite{pomerleau1988alvinn}. It is attractive for its simplicity and because it
needs no reward engineering, but has two well-known weaknesses.

\subsection{Covariate shift.} Because the policy is only ever trained on states visited
by the demonstrator, small errors at run time move the robot into under-represented
states, where it makes larger errors, which compound --- analysed formally by
\textcite{ross2011dagger}, whose DAgger algorithm iteratively queries the expert on
states the learner actually visits. HG-DAgger reduces the expert's burden by taking over
only when the learner is about to fail \parencite{kelly2019hgdagger}. DAgger is not used
here, since the training set was fixed in advance. Action chunking mitigates the problem
partially by committing to temporally consistent multi-step behaviour, which is one
reason it is standard in both ACT and VLAs
\parencite{zhao2023act, li2025qchunking}.

\subsection{Multimodality.} Demonstrations are not a function: the same observation may
be followed by several equally valid actions. How a policy resolves this is determined
by its loss. An $L2$ objective averages the modes, which can produce a physically
invalid action; $L1$ selects one; generative objectives
\parencite{chi2023diffusionpolicy, lipman2023flowmatching} represent the distribution
and sample from it. This is the principal architectural reason modern manipulation
policies are generative, and a property SmolVLA inherits (\refsec{sec:bg-flow}).

\subsection{What the demonstrations contain.} A complementary remedy is to enrich the
demonstration distribution so it covers the states a trained policy actually visits,
including recoveries from error. In the supermarket task this appears as per-episode
positional jitter and as deliberate variety of instruction paraphrases, both of which
broaden the training distribution without an interactive labelling loop.
\textcite{mandlekar2021robomimic} conduct the most systematic study of this question,
finding across several manipulation tasks that dataset composition and observation
design frequently matter more than the choice of learning algorithm --- a conclusion
this thesis reproduces independently in \refChap{chap:results}. The link between
demonstration variety and generalisation recurs throughout the manipulation literature
\parencite{bharadhwaj2024roboagent, black2025pi05, lin2025datascaling}.

\section{Robotic grasping and manipulation}
\label{sec:bg-grasp}
Grasping is the core physical skill in this task. Traditional approaches treat grasp
synthesis as an analytic problem --- computing grasp points that achieve force closure
given object geometry and friction \parencite{bicchi2000grasping} --- whereas
learning-based approaches predict grasps directly from sensor data. Within the learning
family, a useful distinction is between \emph{open-loop grasp detectors}, which propose
a pose from a single observation and hand it to a planner, and \emph{closed-loop
visuomotor policies}, which output actions continuously and correct as they go
\parencite{newbury2023graspreview}. The detector line runs from Dex-Net, which scored
candidates against learned robustness metrics \parencite{mahler2017dexnet}, through
GraspNet-1Billion \parencite{fang2020graspnet} to 6-DoF methods generating grasps from
scene point clouds in clutter \parencite{sundermeyer2021contactgraspnet}. The
closed-loop line begins with systems that learned hand--eye coordination from months of
real grasping attempts \parencite{levine2018handeye} and leads to the imitation-learned
policies studied here.

That taxonomy places this thesis precisely. The scripted expert is analytic: it uses
inverse kinematics to reach a hand-specified top-down grasp pose, reliable because
object models and poses are known in simulation. The learned policy is closed-loop
visuomotor and must infer a grasp from images alone, which is why its grasp success rate
(92.5\%) measures learned competence rather than scripted precision. It performs no
explicit grasp detection, has no notion of force closure, and never represents a grasp
pose as such; whether it genuinely localises the target is what
\refsec{sec:results-lookup} tests.

Two practical considerations shape the design. A two-finger parallel gripper constrains
object geometry: wide or smooth objects slip, which is why the cereal box is excluded
and the water bottle needs a higher grip point (\refChap{chap:methodology}). And the
\emph{approach direction} matters: a top-down grasp followed by frontal extraction was
adopted because the shelf bay is enclosed from above. The per-item variation in success
rate in \refChap{chap:results} tracks both closely.

\section{Mobile manipulation}
\label{sec:bg-mobile}
The embodiment here is a mobile manipulator: an arm carried on a wheeled base. The
literature divides into two approaches. \emph{Decoupled} systems treat navigation and
manipulation separately and sequence them --- drive, park, then pick. \emph{Whole-body}
systems control base and arm jointly, so base motion extends the arm's effective
workspace during the reach. Mobile ALOHA exemplifies the whole-body approach for
imitation learning, collecting demonstrations in which base and arms move together and
showing that co-training with static bimanual data substantially improves the mobile
policy \parencite{fu2024mobilealoha}.

The benchmark literature reflects the same split. HomeRobot poses open-vocabulary mobile
manipulation as an explicit challenge and documents that the bottleneck is usually the
\emph{composition} of navigation and manipulation rather than either alone
\parencite{yenamandra2023homerobot}. TidyBot addresses the complementary problem of
deciding \emph{what} to do, using a language model to infer tidying preferences
\parencite{wu2023tidybot}. RoboCasa likewise places a mobile-base robot in cluttered
household scenes \parencite{nasiriany2024robocasa}.

This thesis takes the decoupled position and studies only the manipulation half: the
base carries the arm and basket and is modelled with its own velocity actuators, but is
held stationary during each pick. Two justifications apply. The research questions
concern language-conditioned grasping under a hardware constraint, and adding base
motion would confound the manipulation result with a navigation result. And holding the
base fixed keeps object--camera geometry constant across episodes, which is necessary
for the displacement experiments of \refsec{sec:results-lookup} to be interpretable. The
cost --- that this is a component of a mobile manipulation system rather than a complete
one --- is stated in \refChap{chap:discussion}, and navigation is set out as future work
in \refsec{sec:future}.

\section{Language grounding for manipulation}
\label{sec:bg-lang}
For a policy to act on ``pick up the milk carton'', it must \emph{ground} the words
in the visual scene. The dominant mechanism is a jointly trained image--text
representation: CLIP established that contrastive pretraining on web-scale
image--caption pairs yields embeddings in which visual and linguistic concepts align
\parencite{radford2021clip}. CLIPort attached such a representation to a manipulation
head \parencite{shridhar2021cliport}; VIMA generalised the interface to multimodal
prompts \parencite{jiang2023vima}; and SayCan \parencite{ahn2022saycan} and Code as
Policies \parencite{liang2023codeaspolicies} used language models to compose skills or
synthesise control programs. In VLAs this grounding is inherited from the pretrained
backbone rather than learned from the small robot dataset, which is what allows a
policy to select the correct object among distractors and respond to unseen paraphrases.

A central empirical question, however, is whether the language is genuinely used or
whether the policy has latched onto a spurious correlate. This is an instance of what
\textcite{geirhos2020shortcut} term \emph{shortcut learning}: a network trained on data
in which two features are confounded learns whichever is easier to extract, and appears
to have acquired the intended capability until the confound is broken by a test the
training data did not contain. Here the confound is between an object's \emph{identity}
and its \emph{location}: if each named object always appears in the same place, then
``reach where the milk is'' and ``reach to $y=-0.26$ when you hear milk'' fit the
demonstrations identically, and the latter is far simpler to learn. Separating them
requires an intervention that decouples the two --- a stronger test than the
instruction-swapping usually reported.

\refsec{sec:results-language} performs the weaker test, holding the scene constant and
varying only the instruction, and finds the reach follows the named item.
\refsec{sec:results-lookup} performs the stronger one, moving items to positions never
used in training, and finds the policy reaches where the item \emph{was} rather than
where it \emph{is}. That the weaker test passes while the stronger fails is exactly the
pattern \textcite{geirhos2020shortcut} describe, which is why both are reported. The
fragility of language conditioning noted by \textcite{kiviranta2025smolvla} makes this
verification important to report rather than assume.

\section{The importance of data and metrics}
\label{sec:bg-data}
A recurring theme is that \emph{data diversity and evaluation quality} can matter as
much as architecture. $\pi_{0.5}$ attributes its generalisation to data diversity rather
than action-model scale \parencite{black2025pi05}; RoboAgent multiplies a small dataset
through semantic augmentation rather than collecting more \parencite{bharadhwaj2024roboagent};
and \textcite{mandlekar2021robomimic} find dataset composition dominating algorithm
choice.

The most directly relevant result is that of \textcite{lin2025datascaling}, who measure
how imitation performance scales along two axes --- the number of \emph{environments and
objects} seen, and the number of demonstrations per environment --- and find
generalisation governed principally by the former. Adding more demonstrations of the
same object in the same place yields rapidly diminishing returns, whereas adding new
objects and placements continues to pay. This is a precise statement of the failure mode
this thesis encounters: the demonstrations used here cover four items at four fixed
positions, so along the axis that drives generalisation the dataset has an effective
size of four. The recall-rather-than-perception finding of \refsec{sec:results-lookup}
is, on this reading, a predicted consequence of the data design rather than a deficiency
of the model.

The community's response at scale has been to pool data across institutions and
embodiments --- BridgeData V2 \parencite{walke2023bridgedata}, Open X-Embodiment
\parencite{padalkar2024openx} and DROID \parencite{khazatsky2024droid} --- and to show
that policies trained on the pool outperform those trained on any one contributor's
data. For a project working with a few hundred self-generated simulated demonstrations
that route is unavailable, which makes the composition of the small dataset that
\emph{is} collected correspondingly more consequential.

\section{Flow-matching action generation}
\label{sec:bg-flow}
The action expert in SmolVLA, like that in $\pi_{0}/\pi_{0.5}$, is a
\emph{flow-matching} model rather than a discrete token predictor
\parencite{black2024pi0, black2025pi05, shukor2025smolvla, zitkovich2023rt2}. Flow
matching \parencite{lipman2023flowmatching} learns a continuous velocity field that
transports a simple noise distribution to the data distribution; at inference the field
is integrated over a small number of steps (ten in the configuration used here). It is
closely related to denoising diffusion \parencite{chi2023diffusionpolicy} but is
formulated as regression onto a velocity along a straight probability path, typically
requiring fewer integration steps --- a material advantage for a controller running at
\SI{20}{Hz}.

Two consequences matter here. Actions are continuous and the full multimodal
distribution is represented rather than collapsed, which suits smooth manipulation and
avoids the mode-averaging failure of regression objectives (\refsec{sec:bg-il}). And
sampling is \emph{stochastic}: repeated queries on the same observation yield slightly
different chunks, so success is inherently a distribution rather than a fixed value.
This directly motivates the evaluation protocol of \refChap{chap:methodology}, which
reports success over many trials with confidence intervals.

\section{Simulation and benchmarks}
\label{sec:bg-sim}
Real-robot data collection is slow, risky and supervision-heavy, so simulation offers a
scalable, safe substitute. A mature benchmark ecosystem exists: Meta-World for
multi-task and meta-learning \parencite{yu2019metaworld}, RLBench for a large suite of
demonstration-backed tasks \parencite{james2020rlbench}, CALVIN for long-horizon
\emph{language-conditioned} manipulation \parencite{mees2022calvin}, and LIBERO for
lifelong learning and knowledge transfer \parencite{liu2023libero}. RoboCasa and
RoboCasa365 are large-scale MuJoCo/robosuite frameworks covering hundreds of household
tasks across thousands of kitchen scenes \parencite{nasiriany2024robocasa,
nasiriany2026robocasa365}.

RoboCasa's reference results use large foundation policies. The preliminary study here
uses it as a stress test of whether \emph{small}, deployable models can perform its
kitchen tasks after task-specific fine-tuning (\refsec{sec:results-journey}); the
negative result motivates the controlled supermarket task. RoboCasa is demanding for a
small model for several compounding reasons: a large and varied object inventory,
cluttered and visually complex scenes, containment reasoning (placing \emph{into} a
cabinet), and a reference set by a foundation model pretrained on a near-identical
distribution. A compact policy must overcome all of these at once, which is why the
sensible response is to reduce difficulty along each axis --- fewer, rigid objects; an
uncluttered scene; a simple open-basket target --- and establish competence there
before reintroducing complexity. The same physics-engine family underpins the custom
environment of \refChap{chap:methodology} \parencite{todorov2012mujoco, zhu2020robosuite}.

The value of simulation is tempered by the \emph{reality gap}: policies can exploit
rendering artefacts, idealised physics or exact object poses that do not hold on
hardware \parencite{zhao2020sim2real}. The dominant mitigation is \emph{domain
randomisation} \parencite{tobin2017domainrand} --- varying textures, lighting, camera
placement and physical parameters so the real world appears as just another variation of
the training distribution. For a VLA there is a subtler gap: the pretrained vision
backbone was trained largely on real photographs, so synthetic renders are themselves
mildly out-of-distribution. Freezing the backbone preserves its real-image prior but
cannot adapt it to the synthetic style; low-rank tuning of the vision layers
\parencite{hu2022lora} is a plausible middle path, discussed as future work. This thesis
does not claim sim-to-real transfer and does not employ domain randomisation, as its aim
is to characterise a policy under a controlled, reproducible task; the pipeline is
nonetheless built on the same tooling a real-robot study would use.

The converse direction is also studied: SIMPLER shows a carefully built simulator can be
used to \emph{evaluate} policies trained on real data, with rankings correlating well
with real-world performance \parencite{li2024simpler} --- though that correlation is
established for one simulator-and-task pairing and cannot simply be assumed here.

\section{Evaluation of manipulation policies}
\label{sec:bg-eval}
A recurring hazard in imitation-learning robotics is optimistic or under-powered
evaluation. Because closed-loop rollouts are expensive, results are often reported over
a handful of trials without confidence intervals, and success is sometimes measured by a
proxy rather than by task completion. The problem is not unique to robotics:
\textcite{henderson2018matters} showed deep reinforcement-learning results were routinely
reported in ways that did not survive replication, and \textcite{agarwal2021precipice}
showed that with typical run counts, point estimates are dominated by sampling noise and
interval estimates are essential rather than decorative.

\subsection{Proxy metrics.} Kiviranta reports that a naively defined success metric and
insufficiently varied data inflate SmolVLA's apparent performance
\parencite{kiviranta2025smolvla}, and the same effect is documented first-hand in the
pilot study (\refsec{sec:results-journey}), where a proximity-based metric overstated a
real 40\% as 80\%. This thesis therefore defines task completion geometrically and
verifies it physically, using the identical criterion at data-collection and evaluation
time.

\subsection{Under-powered samples and interval estimation.} A success rate is a binomial
proportion, and the normal-approximation interval behaves badly for small $n$ and for
proportions near zero or one --- it can extend outside $[0,1]$ and its coverage
oscillates below the nominal level. \textcite{brown2001binomial} recommend the Wilson
score interval \parencite{wilson1927ci} for exactly this regime. Where two policies or
checkpoints are compared on the \emph{same} trial seeds, the observations are paired and
McNemar's test \parencite{mcnemar1947test} applies rather than a two-sample test; with
several comparisons, Holm's correction \parencite{holm1979correction} controls the
family-wise error rate without Bonferroni's conservatism.

\subsection{Checkpoint selection.} Training and even validation loss continue to fall
while closed-loop performance may plateau or regress, so selecting by loss can deploy a
worse policy than an earlier snapshot. The methodology here --- adequate samples, Wilson
intervals, a verified task-completion metric, and selection by closed-loop success ---
is designed to avoid these hazards, and \refsec{sec:results-checkpoint} demonstrates the
effect empirically.

\section{Research gap and positioning}
\label{sec:bg-gap}
The literature establishes that VLAs can transfer web-scale knowledge into control
\parencite{zitkovich2023rt2, brohan2023rt1}; that large VLAs generalise given diverse
data \parencite{black2025pi05, padalkar2024openx}; that compact VLAs can be fine-tuned
on affordable hardware \parencite{shukor2025smolvla, kiviranta2025smolvla,
wen2025tinyvla}; and that at small data scales, dataset composition and evaluation
protocol frequently dominate architecture \parencite{mandlekar2021robomimic,
lin2025datascaling}. Three gaps follow.

\subsection{An end-to-end account under an explicit hardware ceiling.}
Efficiency-focused VLA papers report that their models are cheap to train, but the
constraint is presented as a property of the model rather than the governing constraint
of the study. Under-reported is a rigorously evaluated, end-to-end account of what a
compact VLA can and cannot do when a single-consumer-GPU budget is fixed in advance and
every design decision --- cameras, trainable parameters, image resolution --- must be
made inside it.

\subsection{Testing grounding rather than asserting it.} Language-conditioned policies
are commonly demonstrated by changing the instruction and observing that behaviour
changes. That test cannot distinguish visual grounding from a memorised word-to-location
association, and the shortcut-learning literature \parencite{geirhos2020shortcut}
predicts the latter will be preferred whenever the data permit. Controlled displacement
tests that break the confound are rare, and this thesis reports one over 216 trials.

\subsection{A same-task capacity control.} Claims that a VLA outperforms a smaller
policy are usually made across tasks or papers. Training a non-language policy a
fraction the size on the \emph{identical} dataset, cameras, action space and protocol
isolates what the extra capacity and the language pathway actually buy.
\refsec{sec:results-act} reports such a control, and the answer --- selection rather
than motor competence --- is more specific than the literature usually supports.

Addressing these, the thesis fixes the budget at \SI{12}{GB}, fine-tunes SmolVLA on a
controlled supermarket task, and reports closed-loop success with confidence intervals,
a failure diagnosis, a displacement-based grounding test and a same-task capacity
control. It thereby contributes a first-hand data point --- consistent with $\pi_{0.5}$,
RoboAgent and the data-scaling literature \parencite{black2025pi05,
bharadhwaj2024roboagent, lin2025datascaling} --- that at this scale, evaluation quality
and data variety dominate model choice.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:bg-summary}
Four points carry into the chapters that follow. A VLA is a pretrained
vision--language backbone with an action head, and only its compact members can be
fine-tuned within \SI{12}{GB}, which fixes the model choice. Behaviour cloning from a
scripted expert is subject to covariate shift and to whatever confounds the
demonstration set contains, and action chunking mitigates the first but not the second.
Apparent language grounding may be shortcut learning unless the identity--location
confound is broken by explicit intervention, which dictates the design of
\refsec{sec:results-lookup}. And closed-loop success is a binomial proportion estimated
from expensive trials, so adequate samples, Wilson intervals and paired tests are
requirements rather than refinements. \refChap{chap:methodology} builds the pipeline and
the experimental design on these four foundations.
